I want a robot arm on my desk. Not a toy servo arm that wobbles when you look at it, and not a \$5,000
industrial one either: a real six-axis manipulator that I design, print, wire and program myself, for
**\$500**.

This is the first post in a series where I build exactly that, step by step, with the maths done in Python
along the way. Every figure in this series is generated from code you can run yourself:
[github.com/hfmuzb/python-experiments](https://github.com/hfmuzb/python-experiments).

## the starting point

I began with an industrial-style design spec for a 6-DOF arm: harmonic drives, brushless motors with
field-oriented control, 18-bit encoders, EtherCAT, ROS 2 on a real-time PC. The maths in it (kinematics,
dynamics, trajectory planning) is universal. The hardware is not: priced out, it lands well above \$5,000.

So the plan is to keep the maths and rebuild the hardware around three constraints:

- **\$500 total**, everything included
- **24 V** power
- **mostly 3D printed**, with aluminium only where plastic would fail

:::callout{type="note"}
This is a homemade arm, not a lab instrument. The goal is ±1 mm repeatability, which is enough for
pick-and-place, drawing and stacking things, and is honest about what printed gears can do.
:::

## the spec

- **degrees of freedom:** 6 (base, shoulder, elbow + 3-axis wrist)
- **payload:** 100 g, plus a ~100 g gripper
- **reach:** about 0.40 m to the flange
- **repeatability:** ±1 mm
- **joint speed:** 60 °/s (arm), 90 °/s (wrist)
- **supply:** 24 V DC
- **budget:** \$500

Small numbers on purpose. Every gram at the wrist has to be held up by the shoulder, through a lever arm of
almost half a metre. Keeping the payload light is what makes cheap motors and printed gears viable at all.

## how it fits together

::figure{src="REPLACE_ME/architecture.png" alt="block diagram of the arm's electronics" caption="the whole system: python plans, a microcontroller generates steps, encoders close the loop"}

The architecture in one paragraph: a **Python program on my PC** solves the inverse kinematics and plans
smooth trajectories, then streams joint targets over USB to a **Teensy 4.1**. The Teensy turns them into
step pulses for six **TMC2209 stepper drivers**. Each stepper drives its joint through a **3D-printed
reduction** (cycloidal drives for the big joints, belts and small planetary gears for the wrist). A cheap
**AS5600 magnetic encoder** on every joint output reports the real angle, so the controller can catch skipped
steps and gear backlash.

The big trade-offs compared with the industrial spec:

- **harmonic drives → printed cycloidal drives.** A harmonic drive alone costs more than the whole budget.
- **brushless + FOC → steppers.** Steppers hold position open-loop and cost about \$12 each.
- **18-bit encoders → 12-bit AS5600.** Roughly 0.6 mm per count at full reach, inside our ±1 mm goal.
- **brakes on the shoulder → counterbalance spring.** It stops the arm dropping when power is off, and frees
  up torque.
- **ROS 2 on real-time Linux → plain Python.** The microcontroller does the real-time work, so the PC doesn't
  have to.

One design rule matters more than any of these: **keep the motors close to the base.** The elbow motor
sits at the shoulder and drives the elbow through a belt. A motor at the elbow would weigh about 0.3 kg at
the end of the upper arm, and the shoulder would have to lift it on every move.

## the geometry: Denavit–Hartenberg parameters

The arm has the classic "anthropomorphic arm + spherical wrist" layout: three joints position the wrist,
and three wrist axes that meet at a single point orient the gripper. That intersection matters. It splits
inverse kinematics into a position problem and an orientation problem, and both can be solved in closed
form. That's the next post.

To describe the geometry, I use the standard **Denavit–Hartenberg (DH)** convention. Each joint gets a
coordinate frame, and four numbers describe how to get from one frame to the next:

$$
A_i^{i-1} =
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\theta_i & -\sin\theta_i\cos\alpha_i & \sin\theta_i\sin\alpha_i & a_i\cos\theta_i \\
\sin\theta_i & \cos\theta_i\cos\alpha_i & -\cos\theta_i\sin\alpha_i & a_i\sin\theta_i \\
0 & \sin\alpha_i & \cos\alpha_i & d_i \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Here $\theta_i$ is the joint angle, $d_i$ the offset along the previous joint axis, $a_i$ the link length,
and $\alpha_i$ the twist between consecutive axes. For this arm:

$$
\begin{array}{llcccc}
i & \text{joint} & d_i\ (\text{m}) & a_i\ (\text{m}) & \alpha_i & \text{range} \\
\hline
1 & \text{base} & 0.120 & 0 & +90^\circ & \pm 160^\circ \\
2 & \text{shoulder} & 0 & 0.180 & 0 & -10^\circ \dots +170^\circ \\
3 & \text{elbow} & 0 & 0 & +90^\circ & -135^\circ \dots +90^\circ \\
4 & \text{forearm roll} & 0.160 & 0 & -90^\circ & \pm 170^\circ \\
5 & \text{wrist pitch} & 0 & 0 & +90^\circ & \pm 110^\circ \\
6 & \text{flange roll} & 0.050 & 0 & 0 & \pm 180^\circ
\end{array}
$$

The elbow joint has a $+90^\circ$ offset, and the forearm length sits in $d_4$ rather than $a_3$. That's
standard for a spherical wrist, and it's an easy detail to get wrong.

In code, the transform and the chain of frames are only a few lines:

```python
def dh_transform(theta, d, a, alpha):
    ct, st = np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)
    ca, sa = np.cos(alpha), np.sin(alpha)
    return np.array([
        [ct, -st * ca,  st * sa, a * ct],
        [st,  ct * ca, -ct * sa, a * st],
        [0.0,      sa,       ca,      d],
        [0.0,     0.0,      0.0,    1.0],
    ])


def frames(q, links=ARM):
    T = np.eye(4)
    out = [T]
    for qi, link in zip(q, links, strict=True):
        T = T @ dh_transform(qi + link.offset, link.d, link.a, link.alpha)
        out.append(T)
    return out
```

Multiplying all six gives the pose of the flange relative to the base, $T_6^0$. I checked this
implementation against [Robotics Toolbox for Python](https://github.com/petercorke/robotics-toolbox-python)
on 200 random poses, and the two agree to $10^{-12}$.

## where the money goes

::figure{src="REPLACE_ME/budget.png" alt="horizontal bar chart of the budget by category" caption="planned budget at aliexpress-level prices, with $40 kept for mistakes"}

Motors are the single biggest line, but the less exciting items (filament, bearings, aluminium parts,
fasteners) add up to more. The \$40 contingency isn't optional. The first printed gearbox will not be the
last one.

The aluminium is limited to three places where plastic genuinely fails: the shoulder output hub, which
carries the biggest bending load; the eccentric shafts of the cycloidal drives, which need about 0.05 mm
precision; and the base turntable plate, whose stiffness sets the accuracy of everything above it.

## the roadmap

The first articles need no hardware at all. Everything gets designed and checked in simulation while parts
are on their way:

1. **this post:** requirements, architecture, geometry
2. **kinematics:** forward and inverse kinematics (all 8 solutions), the Jacobian, and singularities
3. **dynamics:** how much torque each joint really needs, and how a spring can do half the work
4. **trajectory planning:** smooth motion that steppers can follow without skipping
5. **drivetrain and parts:** choosing motors and gear ratios, the final bill of materials
6. **printing a cycloidal gearbox:** generating the gear profile in Python, then testing it
7. **electronics and safety**
8. **firmware:** steppers with an encoder loop on top
9. **host software:** teach-and-replay and straight-line moves
10. **calibration**
11. **validation:** does it actually hit ±1 mm?

Next up: kinematics. Given a target position and orientation for the gripper, which joint angles get us
there? It turns out there are up to eight answers.
